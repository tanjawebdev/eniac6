# ENIAC Six introduction

Eight automatic scenes, approximately 2 minutes 13 seconds in total. English copy and reading times live in `introStory.ts`; vector illustrations live in `IntroIllustrations.tsx`. Portraits reuse the six existing images in `client/public/portraits`. All assets work locally without external requests.

## Controls

- INTRO starts the guide from the beginning, including when it is already playing or paused.
- HOME and “Skip intro” return to the installation. An existing cable connection can then open its chapter through the existing hardware logic.
- Back, Pause/Resume, Next and the eight progress segments allow manual navigation. The final action is “Begin exploring”; automatic playback also returns to the installation after the final scene.
- Keyboard: left/right arrows, Space to pause/resume, Escape to exit. Focused buttons keep their normal keyboard behaviour.
- Playback pauses while the browser tab is hidden. Reduced-motion preferences disable illustration and slide motion. Pausing freezes repeated illustrative motion but allows text and portraits to finish appearing when a different chapter is selected.
- New physical cable connections retain the existing behaviour of opening the corresponding chapter immediately.

The portrait layout follows the installation’s 2160 × 3840 canvas, including the existing S-key developer fit mode. A landscape layout supports desktop previews.

## Historical basis

Sources checked 2 September 2026. These are editorial references; the installation remains an uninterrupted local experience.

1. [Smithsonian, ENIAC Accumulator #2](https://www.si.edu/object/eniac-accumulator-2%3Anmah_334742): room-sized machine, approximately 18,000 vacuum tubes, Army commission for artillery firing tables, and the existing human-computing workforce. “Vacuum tubes” replaces “lamps”; 19,000 is not used.
2. [University of Pennsylvania, On ENIAC’s anniversary, a nod to its female ‘computers’](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/eniacs-anniversary-nod-its-female-computers): 40 original panels, the six programmers, the 1997 Hall of Fame induction, and the distinction between wartime development and postwar completion/demonstration.
3. [University of Pennsylvania, The world’s first general purpose computer turns 75](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/news/worlds-first-general-purpose-computer-turns-75): ENIAC’s general-purpose electronic computing role, physical programming, and the lack of recognition for the women’s work. It gives a more precise tube count of 17,468; the introductory display uses the conventional rounded figure “≈18,000”.
4. [ENIAC Programmers Project, Documentary info](https://eniacprogrammers.org/documentary-info/): the programmers’ development of the ballistics program without programming languages or manuals, their exclusion from the 1946 public introduction, and recorded oral histories.
5. [IEEE Spectrum, The Computers Who Brought ENIAC to Life](https://spectrum.ieee.org/eniac-woman-programmers): interview with ENIAC researcher Kathy Kleiman about wartime recruitment, mathematical work and the selection of six women from the human-computing workforce.
6. [National Women’s History Museum, Women and Computing](https://www.womenshistory.org/articles/women-and-computing): education and employment context. The copy says “mathematical training” rather than asserting that all six graduated in mathematics.

The copy describes an expansion of opportunities during WWII, not the first time women held technical jobs. It describes their ENIAC contribution as largely overlooked for decades, rather than claiming nobody recognised any of their work before 1997. The subtitle qualifies “first computer” as electronic and general-purpose; the machine scene also specifies digital.

Illustrations are schematic, not archival evidence. The cabinet drawing evokes ENIAC rather than reconstructing all 40 panels. The firing-table values and trajectory curves are illustrative. The contemporary card, cable and controls diagrams explain this installation, not ENIAC’s historical operating procedure. The four control labels in the final illustration match the Programming chapter’s existing configuration. Changing these controls changes visual parameters; it does not rewrite historical ENIAC programs.
